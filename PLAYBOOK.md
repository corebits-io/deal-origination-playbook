# The Deal Origination Playbook for Private Equity

The method, in six steps. Read this whether or not you install anything — the
package in this repo just runs what is written here.

---

## The problem this solves

Ask most firms how they find deals and the answer is some mix of four things:

- Banker relationships
- Paid databases
- Conferences
- A couple of associates doing outbound when they have time

Every one of those channels shows you the same population as everybody else.
Bankers bring processes, which means competition and a price discovered against
other buyers. Databases sell the same rows to every subscriber. Conferences
surface the companies that market themselves. Ad-hoc outbound covers whatever
the associate happened to search on Tuesday.

None of that is wrong. It is just not coverage. The companies that never enter
any of those channels — no banker, no listing, no booth, no press — are the
population a proprietary process exists to find. They are also, structurally,
the ones you buy at a sensible price, because you are the only one in the
conversation.

**The edge is not a better database. It is systematic coverage of a market you
have deliberately defined, worked in a fixed order, with evidence attached to
every claim.**

That is what follows.

---

## The order matters

```
1. Map the market          →  what does the population actually look like?
2. Find the companies      →  enumerate it from public sources
3. Establish ownership     →  who can actually sell?
4. Score against mandate   →  which of these do you want?
5. Read the signals        →  which of those might move, and when?
6. Owner + brief           →  who do you talk to, and what do you know?
```

Each step's output is the next step's input. Running them out of order is the
single most common reason origination effort produces nothing: a list built
before the market is mapped is a list for a market you have not defined, and
scoring it just sorts noise.

---

# Step 1 — Map the market beyond the obvious databases

**Goal:** understand the shape of the population before you spend a single hour
enumerating it.

A market map is not a list. It is a page that answers:

**Segments.** How does this sector actually divide? Not by SIC code — by how
the businesses run. Commercial versus residential. Contract-recurring versus
project. Regulated versus not. Owner-operated versus managed. The segment
boundaries decide which companies are comparable, and they rarely match the
industry classifications a database sorts by.

**Size distribution.** Where does the revenue sit? A sector with 4,000
sub-$2M operators and 30 companies above $50M is a different origination
problem from one with 300 companies clustered at $20M. The first needs a
roll-up thesis or a much lower size floor. Knowing this before you build a list
stops you sourcing a population you cannot transact with.

**Fragmentation and who is consolidating.** Read competing sponsors' portfolio
pages and add-on announcements. This tells you three things at once: which
companies are already gone, which thesis your competitors are running, and what
profile they pay for. In a heavily consolidated sector, most of your naive
keyword matches will already be somebody's add-on.

**The typical owner.** First generation or second? Trade background or
professional management? What age does the sector's ownership skew to, based on
what owners have said publicly? This shapes both your fit criteria and how the
first conversation should go.

**Where the companies are findable.** This is the part the database skips. Every
sector has its own discovery surface: licensing boards for regulated trades,
certification registries for manufacturing, integration marketplaces for
vertical software, association member directories almost everywhere. Identify
that surface per sector before you start looking, and write it down.

**How to build the map:** three to six hours of reading, using association
publications, trade press, sponsor portfolio pages, the largest players' own
sites, and one or two industry participant interviews if you can get them. Then
write it as a page and share it internally. If two people on your team would
describe the sector differently, you are not done.

> **The test of a good map:** it tells you where *not* to look. A map that
> makes every company in the sector sound like a target has not mapped
> anything.

# Step 2 — Find the companies that actually fit the mandate

**Goal:** enumerate the mapped population from public sources, with a source
recorded for every row.

The best sources for private companies are free, public, and ignored — because
everyone else bought the same subscription. Companies appear in these sources
because they *operate*, not because they market.

**Tier 1 — free, public, high signal**

| Source | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Industry association member directories | Membership means an established operator paying dues. Usually lists location and specialty |
| Trade show exhibitor lists | Public, current, self-selected for companies healthy enough to buy a booth. Old years are usually still online, which gives you tenure |
| Government registries | US Secretary of State registries, UK Companies House. Formation dates, officers, and in the UK, actual ownership via the PSC register |
| Licensing boards | Contractors, electricians, plumbers, pharmacies, home care, funeral homes. Public rosters with license dates |
| Certification registries | ISO, AS9100 and sector equivalents. Proves an operating standard, with a date |
| Sponsor portfolio pages | Who is already gone, and which independents sit around each platform |

**Tier 2 — public, noisier, still free**

Review platforms and map density sweeps (review count and oldest review age are
rough proxies for scale and tenure in local services), job boards (a company
hiring technicians is operating), local business journal "fastest growing" and
"family business" lists, anniversary and award coverage in local press.

**Tier 3 — disciplined generic search**

The fallback where a sector has no directory culture. Query patterns like
`"[sector]" "family owned" [region]`, `"celebrating * years" "[sector]"`,
`"[sector]" "second generation"`.

**Work several angles.** Each angle is blind to what the others surface. Good
private companies are frequently visible in exactly one place. One angle run
well still leaves most of the market invisible.

**Dedupe on domain, not name.** Trades and local services reuse names
constantly. Appearing in three independent sources is itself a mild positive
signal — record it.

**What to avoid:** bought lists of "off-market businesses for sale" (recycled,
stale, and sold to every buyer who paid the same vendor — if a list is for sale
to you, it is not proprietary), scraped third-party exports, and anyone selling
"verified owner emails."

**Target size:** 100 to 300 genuinely qualified companies for one thesis in one
sector. A list of 2,000 unscreened rows is worse than 150 good ones. It buries
the dozen real succession conversations inside add-ons and franchisees, and
every wasted send spends your name in a market where owners talk to each other.

# Step 3 — Identify founder and family-owned businesses

**Goal:** establish who owns each company, because that decides who can sell it
and how the process will run.

Most bad target lists are bad because ownership was guessed once, early, and
every later step inherited the guess. A perfect approach to a company that
structurally cannot sell to you is wasted twice — on the effort, and on the
false pipeline entry.

| Ownership type | Who can sell | Actionable? |
|---|---|---|
| **founder-owned** | The founder. One decision maker | Yes — the core target |
| **family-owned** | Depends: one patriarch, or five siblings with equal stakes and unequal interest. Finding out *is* the research task | Yes, with patience |
| **partner-owned** | All partners, together, under an agreement you will not see | Yes, score the complexity |
| **PE-backed (platform)** | The sponsor, on the sponsor's timeline, in a banked process | No — market intelligence only |
| **PE-backed (add-on)** | The sponsor, not the founder you found on LinkedIn | No |
| **VC-backed** | Founders plus a board they do not control, over a preference stack | Rarely |
| **corporate-owned** | The parent's corp dev, never the enthusiastic GM | Only if carve-outs are in the mandate |
| **ESOP** | The trustee, at fair market value, through a formal process | Yes, for the patient |
| **franchisee** | The franchisee, subject to franchisor consent and an ROFR | Only for a franchise roll-up |
| **public** | Not applicable here — disclosure and takeover regimes | No |

**The distinction that breaks most lists:**

> A company that **is** an independent business can sell itself to you.
> A company that **was** independent and is now a sponsor's add-on cannot, no
> matter what its website still says.

Add-ons keep their founder-era name, site, and "family owned since 1987" copy
for years after the sale. That copy scores highest on naive family-owned
matching, which is exactly why the sponsor screen has to be explicit and has to
run before scoring — press releases, sponsor portfolio pages, recapitalization
announcements.

**Ownership is a fact to verify, never a vibe to infer from design quality.** If
you cannot establish it from a registry, a filing, a press release, or the
company's own statements, the value is `Not found` and the row is flagged for
manual review. `Not found` is a normal outcome on 10 to 20 percent of rows.

# Step 4 — Score targets against the investment thesis

**Goal:** one bucket per company, one reason string per bucket, applied
identically across the list.

**Four buckets, and nothing else:**

- **Strong Fit** — inside the box on every dimension you can verify, *and*
  carrying a documented signal that makes it more than a category match. The
  signal must be quotable with a source
- **Fit** — inside the box, nothing contradicting, no specific signal beyond
  category membership
- **Near Miss** — fails one soft criterion, or a key criterion is `Not found`
  rather than failed. Kept, because theses widen and companies grow
- **Not a Fit** — fails a hard exclusion. Record which rule fired

**The hard rules that matter most:**

1. **Sponsor-owned is Not a Fit.** The single most common miss.
2. **Competitor conflicts are Not a Fit.** Two shapes: owned by a competing
   sponsor in your sector, or competing with your own portfolio. Never research
   or contact the second under cover of buying interest.
3. **A thin website is not a negative signal.** This rule has the highest cost
   when broken. Surface-signal scoring inverts the result in this market: the
   best-run 30-year-old business in the sector often has the worst website, and
   the venture-funded competitor burning cash has the best one.
4. **Never infer financials from headcount and present them as fact.** Revenue
   per employee varies by an order of magnitude across sectors and several times
   within one. Estimate if you want, but label it and state the basis.
5. **Unknown is not failed.** A `Not found` revenue is not a failed size test.
6. **Ownership type gates everything.** If it is unactionable, no other virtue
   rescues the row. If it is `Not found`, the row cannot be Strong Fit.
7. **Every rubric change re-runs the whole list.** A list scored under two
   rubrics is two lists interleaved.

**Default direction:** when genuinely torn between Fit and Near Miss, choose
Fit. A slightly loose list costs you a few ignored letters. A tight filter
applied to thin information costs you the owner who would have taken the call.

**Then audit, every single run.** Twenty minutes:

1. Pull the 10 highest-confidence Strong Fits and verify the quoted signal
   actually appears at the source URL
2. Pull 20 random Not a Fits and look for false negatives — the expensive
   failure, and invisible unless you look
3. Re-run the sponsor and competitor screen against the keep pile
4. Sanity check the distribution. 80 percent Strong Fit means the rubric is
   flattering you. 5 percent surviving means either the sourcing or the rubric
   is wrong

# Step 5 — Read the signals

**Goal:** separate "fits the mandate" from "might actually move," using things
owners said in public.

A fit score tells you whether you want the company. A signal tells you whether
a conversation is likely to go anywhere in the next eighteen months. They are
different questions and conflating them is how a list of good companies produces
no meetings.

**Signals worth more than any keyword match:**

- 20+ years in business with no visible successor in the leadership team
- Retirement language in an interview, award profile, or association bio
- The owner stepping back to Chairman while a non-family GM runs daily ops
- A second generation visibly entering the business — or visibly absent from it
- Long-held association board seats or officer roles handed over
- A second location or expansion recently wound down or sold
- Ownership-change filings: registry officer changes, PSC updates, new
  registered agents
- Real operating change: a new facility, a certification added, a hiring burst

**Neutral, despite what list vendors claim:**

- A dated website. Good private companies have terrible websites
- No LinkedIn presence. Normal for owners over 55 in most trades
- Small stated headcount. Says nothing about revenue or quality

**The evidence standard:** quote it, source it, date it. If you cannot quote it,
you do not have it. A 2018 award profile describes a 2018 company, so
`evidence_date` matters as much as the signal itself.

**And the line you do not cross:** a signal is something a person said or filed
in public, in a professional context. It is not an inference about their health,
their family, their finances, or their age from a photograph. Public
professional sources only, no pretexting, no personal-life data. Beyond the
ethics, inferred signals are usually wrong, and acting on one in a first
conversation with an owner is unrecoverable.

# Step 6 — Find the owner, then research the business

**Goal:** the person who can say yes, a route that reaches them, and a page of
real knowledge about their company before you say a word.

### Find the right person first, before looking for any address

Order matters. An email for the wrong person is worth less than nothing.

1. Owner / Founder / President
2. Majority shareholder or managing partner
3. CEO — only where the CEO also holds equity
4. The family member running operations, in a family business, even if they are
   not the largest holder. They are the gateway to the rest
5. CFO — a useful entry at larger targets, never the decision maker

**A hired CEO cannot sell a family business.** Writing to one wastes the first
touch and often delivers your name to the owner with a gatekeeper's framing.

Look at the company's own site first, then registry and Companies House filings
(officers and registered agents are public and reliable), then LinkedIn, then
licensing boards, association listings, and local press.

**One contact per company.** Small companies talk. Messaging the owner and the
owner's son in the same week reads as a mail merge to both.

### Verify the route honestly

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `verified` | Confirmed deliverable by a verification service |
| `published` | On the company's own site or an official filing |
| `Not found` | No email located. Honest and common |

Never guess an address from a pattern. Pattern-guessing is the fastest way to
destroy a sending domain, and a high bounce rate on a young domain can end your
ability to send email at all. One guessed address is not worth that.

A warm introduction outperforms the best cold message you will ever send, so
check for a shared advisor, banker, accountant, or peer before going cold.
For offline owners, a published phone number or a posted letter is a real route,
not a fallback of last resort — in some sectors it is the best one.

### Then write the one-page brief

Before any first conversation, one page per target:

- What the business actually does, in the segment language from the market map
- Scale evidence: what is published, what is estimated, and the basis
- Ownership, verified, with the filing or statement behind it
- Tenure and history — founding date, generations, locations
- The signal, quoted and dated
- Two or three specifics only someone who read about them would know
- What you do not know, listed explicitly

The last line is the important one. A brief that hides its gaps produces a
confident first conversation built on an invention, and owners in small
industries remember that.

**This is where the playbook stops.** Outreach is a separate discipline with its
own rules, its own compliance surface, and its own package.

---

## What good looks like

| Measure | A realistic first cycle |
|---|---|
| Sectors mapped | 1, properly |
| Companies enumerated | 100–300 |
| Sponsor-owned screened out | 15–40% of raw finds, sector dependent |
| Strong Fit + Fit surviving | 30–60% of the list |
| Ownership established | 80–90% of the keep pile |
| Owner identified | ~80% of the keep pile |
| Sendable route (verified or published) | 40–60% of those |
| `Not found` fields | Everywhere, constantly, correctly |

Time: a few days of real work, not one afternoon. Real research is slow and
speed here comes directly out of accuracy.

---

## The five ways this goes wrong

1. **Skipping the map.** Sourcing before defining produces a list nobody can
   act on and nobody can audit.
2. **Optimizing for row count.** 1,500 scraped rows lose to 150 sourced ones on
   every metric that matters, including meetings booked.
3. **Guessing ownership.** One early guess propagates through every later step.
4. **Scoring on surface signals.** Website quality, branding, and LinkedIn
   polish are inversely correlated with what you want in this market.
5. **Letting the machine fill gaps.** A confident wrong revenue figure, owner
   name, or email address costs more than the blank it replaced. `Not found` is
   always the better answer.
